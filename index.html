Biology of History and Adaptation

How biological systems remember, adapt, and survive.

I study the biology of cellular history and adaptation. How do biological systems remember previous events and their consequences? And how do they use this memory for adaptation and survival?

01

How is biological memory formed?

Biological systems experience signals that may be mechanical, chemical, physical, infectious, or a combination of these signals. How do these signals induce a new biological state that persists even after the signal has been removed? In general, we call this memory. It may be immune, mechanical, metabolic, or exist across different levels, from the cell to the organ. How are these memories stored?

02

How does memory influence adaptation?

How do these memories influence the present and future of the system? Does memory always have an advantageous effect, or can it sometimes have deleterious consequences? We also call this maladaptation. What factors affect this memory-driven new biological state?

03

Can maladaptive memory be reset?

If these memories have deleterious consequences, can they be reset by resetting the altered memory?

Human Behaviour

Understanding people.

Understanding how people think, behave, adapt, and interact within their social environment.

Humans do not live alone. We live in a niche—a society of other humans. Evolutionarily, humans seek security, accumulate food and other resources for the future, and interact with others based on their interests.

01

What drives human behaviour?

How do security, resources, interests, relationships, and our social environment shape the way people think and behave?

02

How does human behaviour change within a power structure?

People behave differently depending on where they stand within a social or institutional structure. Can we identify patterns in their actions, understand their interests, and ask better questions of those who hold power or influence the system?

03

Can understanding human behaviour make our social world more predictable?

We always deal with other humans—in our families, workplaces, society, laboratories, and friendships. If we understand how people behave, can we better prepare ourselves for the social landscape around us?

Scientific Writing

The craft of scientific writing.

Writing is not only a way to communicate ideas. It is also a way to think clearly and generate new ideas.

Writing is one skill that is involved not only in professional success, but also in personal growth. We write grants, manuscripts, research papers, review articles, and much more.

But writing does more than communicate our thoughts. It reduces the noise in our thinking. Writing helps us think clearly, organize our thoughts, and generate new ideas.

People often focus more on speaking, but I think writing is an essential skill, particularly for non-native English speakers. The ability to put an idea into clear and precise words can make a significant difference in how that idea is understood.

I will regularly write blog posts about scientific writing, how to think through writing, and how we can improve the way we communicate scientific ideas.

01

Can writing help us think more clearly?

Writing is not simply a way of communicating what we already know. How does putting our thoughts into words help reduce the noise, organize our thinking, and generate new ideas?

02

Why is writing an essential scientific skill?

Scientists write grants, manuscripts, research papers, reviews, and many other forms of scientific communication. How can better writing help us communicate our ideas more clearly and effectively?

03

How can we become better scientific writers?

Scientific writing can be particularly challenging for non-native English speakers. What can we learn, practice, and change to make our scientific writing clearer, more precise, and more meaningful?

About

Who am I?

I am an independent researcher exploring biology and society through an engineering lens.

I am trained as a chemical engineer. I completed my doctorate at IIT Bombay, India, where I studied the long-term expansion and maintenance of human mesenchymal stem cells on soft biomaterial surfaces. I showed that soft surfaces not only maintain stemness but also delay replicative senescence.

An interesting finding from my PhD work was that cells remember and maintain their proliferative and morphological phenotype when rewired on a soft surface and then moved to tissue culture plastic. These findings guided me to pursue my postdoctoral research on the mechanics of senescence and on developing methods to rejuvenate senescent cells instead of eliminating them.

I have continued to explore the idea of cellular mechanical memory. Like humans, cells can remember past events and use that information to adapt and survive when they encounter similar signals in the future.

Mechanical memory, however, may be only one form of biological memory. Biological systems can have immune, metabolic, and mechanical memories. When cells encounter these signals, they can undergo changes in epigenetic and gene-expression programs. Some of this information can be stored through changes in chromatin and gene expression, transforming cells into a new biological state.

This raises a question that I find particularly interesting: Can a transformed cellular state be reversed by erasing or resetting these memories and returning the cell to a more naïve state?

I am also interested in helping people like me—people who may not come from a very strong or privileged background—understand human behaviour and develop scientific writing skills. Writing is a powerful tool for expressing our thoughts, asking better questions, and making our ideas understandable to others.

Currently, I am developing projects exploring the potential role of mechanosignaling in obesity, weight gain and weight loss, and cellular senescence.
